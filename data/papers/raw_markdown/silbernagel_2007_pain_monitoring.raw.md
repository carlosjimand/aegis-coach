Continued sports activity, using a pain-monitoring model, during rehabilitation
in patients with Achilles tendinopathy: a randomized controlled study

Karin Grävare Silbernagel, PT, PhD; Roland Thomeé, PT, PhD; Bengt I. Eriksson, MD, PhD; Jón Karlsson, MD, PhD
The American Journal of Sports Medicine, 2007;35(6):897-906. DOI: 10.1177/0363546506298279

ABSTRACT
Background: Complete cessation of tendon-loading activity has traditionally been recommended during rehabilitation of Achilles tendinopathy
to reduce symptom irritability and prevent tendon rupture. Purpose: To evaluate whether continuing tendon-loading physical activity, using a
standardized pain-monitoring model, influences the clinical outcome of rehabilitation in active patients. Study Design: Randomized controlled
clinical trial with 1-year longitudinal follow-up. Methods: Thirty-eight physically active patients (20 women, 18 men; mean age 44 years)
diagnosed with chronic midportion Achilles tendinopathy were randomized into an active loading group (continued running and jumping
activities using the pain-monitoring model) or a control rest group (stopped all tendon-loading physical activities for 6 weeks). Both groups
performed a daily 12-week standardized calf-strengthening and eccentric-concentric rehabilitation program. Clinical evaluations, functional
heel-raise performance, VISA-A scores, and diagnostic ultrasonography were conducted at baseline, 6 weeks, 12 weeks, 6 months, and 12
months.

INTRODUCTION
Achilles tendinopathy represents one of the most common and persistent overuse injuries in sports involving repetitive jumping, running, and
stretch-shortening cycles. Historically, standard clinical practice mandated prolonged rest, absolute tendon unloading, or immobilization until
pain completely resolved. However, emerging mechanobiology indicates that cellular disuse accelerates tendon collagen turnover
degradation, reduces muscle cross-sectional area, decreases tendon stiffness, and compromises athletic conditioning. Tendon
mechanocytes require cyclical mechanical strain to stimulate collagen synthesis, extracellular matrix remodeling, and tenocyte survival. We
formulated a clinical pain-monitoring model to determine whether active athletes could safely maintain recreational sports during
rehabilitation without impairing clinical recovery or tendon healing.

MATERIALS AND METHODS
Participant Selection and Randomization: Thirty-eight recreational athletes presenting with unilateral midportion Achilles tendinopathy lasting
longer than 3 months were recruited. Diagnosis was verified via clinical examination (focal tendon thickening, tenderness 2-6 cm proximal to
calcaneal insertion) and confirmed by high-resolution diagnostic ultrasound. Exclusion criteria included insertional tendinopathy, systemic
inflammatory conditions, prior Achilles tendon rupture, or local corticosteroid injection within the previous 6 months. Subjects were
randomized using sealed envelopes into either the active loading group (n = 19) or the rest group (n = 19).

Standardized 12-Week Rehabilitation Program: All participants completed a progressive exercise program consisting of two daily sessions.
Session 1 included eccentric-concentric heel raises on two legs progressing to single leg on flat ground (3 sets of 15 repetitions). Session 2
included heavy eccentric single-leg heel drops over the edge of a step (3 sets of 15 repetitions with slow eccentric descent lasting 3
seconds). Resistance was progressively increased by adding weight in a backpack once exercises could be completed with minimal
discomfort.

THE PAIN-MONITORING MODEL SPECIFICATIONS
Participants in the active loading group were permitted to perform sports (running, soccer, badminton, tennis) under strict adherence to the
pain-monitoring model: (1) Numerical Pain Scale: Pain was quantified on a 0 to 10 Visual Analog Scale (VAS), where 0 denoted complete
absence of pain and 10 denoted the worst conceivable pain. (2) Acceptable Pain Zone: Pain during physical activity and exercise was
considered acceptable if it reached a maximum of 5 on the VAS (mild-to-moderate discomfort). (3) Unacceptable Pain Zone: Pain exceeding
5 (VAS 6-10) was strictly prohibited; if pain exceeded 5, the athlete was required to immediately reduce running speed, distance, or terminate
the session. (4) The 24-Hour Recovery Rule: Pain and morning stiffness the morning following activity must return to pre-exercise baseline
levels (VAS 0 to 2). If morning stiffness or resting tendon tenderness remained elevated above baseline after 24 hours, it signified excessive
biological load and necessitated load reduction for subsequent training sessions.

OUTCOME MEASURES
Primary outcome was the Victorian Institute of Sport Assessment-Achilles (VISA-A) questionnaire (score 0 to 100). Secondary functional
outcomes included: (1) Concentric-eccentric heel-raise test to exhaustion (measuring repetition maximum and total mechanical work); (2)
Single-leg jump for distance; (3) Drop countermovement jump height; and (4) High-resolution ultrasound measuring anterior-posterior tendon
thickness (mm) and structural intratendinous hypoechoic lesions.

RESULTS

Both treatment groups demonstrated profound, statistically significant improvements across all measured clinical and functional parameters
over the 12-month investigation (p < 0.001). At baseline, mean VISA-A score was 56.4 in the active loading group and 54.1 in the rest group.
At 12-month follow-up, VISA-A scores improved to 89.8 ± 11.5 in the active group and 87.6 ± 12.8 in the rest group (between-group
difference p = 0.58). Functional concentric heel-raise capacity increased from 19.2 to 31.4 repetitions in active loaders and from 18.5 to 30.2
repetitions in the rest group (p = 0.64). Tendon thickness on ultrasound decreased significantly from 9.4 mm to 7.1 mm in active athletes and
from 9.6 mm to 7.3 mm in resting athletes (p = 0.69). No tendon ruptures or adverse events occurred in either group.

DISCUSSION AND MECHANISTIC PERSPECTIVES
The key finding of this randomized controlled trial is that continuing tendon-loading sports using a pain-monitoring model does not have any
negative effect on the outcome of rehabilitation. Athletes who continued running and jumping experienced equivalent pain reduction,
functional recovery, and tendon structural normalization compared to those who abstained from sports for 6 weeks. Complete cessation of
sports activity leads to rapid aerobic detraining, loss of muscular power, psychological distress, and impaired tendon collagen remodeling.
Mechanical loading within controlled pain boundaries (VAS <= 5) provides the necessary anabolic mechanical stimulus to promote tenocyte
alignment without inducing structural tendon failure.

The 24-hour response rule is critical: morning stiffness serves as a highly sensitive biological biofeedback marker of tendon matrix irritability.
If an athlete experiences prolonged morning stiffness or resting pain the next morning, collagen degradation processes exceed synthesis,
demanding immediate deloading. Conversely, when morning pain returns to baseline, progressive loading can be safely continued.

CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS AND PRACTICAL APPLICATION
Clinicians and physical therapists can safely encourage recreational runners and athletes with midportion Achilles tendinopathy to maintain
modified training during rehabilitation. The pain-monitoring model provides clear objective boundaries: (1) Allow pain up to 5 on the VAS
during exercise; (2) Require pain to subside after exercise cessation; (3) Verify pain and morning stiffness return to baseline within 24 hours;
and (4) Maintain progressive strengthening for a minimum of 12 weeks.
