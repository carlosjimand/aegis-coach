A Proposed Return-to-Sport Program for Patients With Midportion Achilles
Tendinopathy: Rationale and Implementation

Karin Grävare Silbernagel, PT, ATC, PhD; Kay M. Crossley, BAppSc(Physio), PhD
Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy, 2015;45(11):876-886. DOI: 10.2519/jospt.2015.5887

ABSTRACT
Achilles tendinopathy is a frequent, debilitating condition among recreational runners, track athletes, and field-sport competitors.
Management requires structured mechanical loading to stimulate tendon matrix remodeling and tensile capacity restoration while preventing
symptom exacerbation. This clinical commentary presents an evidence-informed 4-phase return-to-sport program for patients with midportion
Achilles tendinopathy. The model incorporates a pain-monitoring model (VAS 0 to 5 out of 10), progressive heavy slow resistance (HSR),
stretch-shortening cycle (SSC) plyometrics, and sport-specific clearance testing. Clinicians are guided through criteria-based progression
models rather than arbitrary time-based milestones.

INTRODUCTION
Midportion Achilles tendinopathy is characterized by localized tendon pain, focal morning stiffness, and impaired triceps surae plantar flexor
performance. Pathophysiologically, failed healing responses lead to tendon matrix disorganization, hypervascularity, and neurovascular
ingrowth. Tendon tissue exhibits high mechanosensitivity, requiring optimal mechanical loading to stimulate tenocyte protein synthesis and
extracellular matrix realignment. Complete rest and tendon unloading are contraindicated as they accelerate tendon disuse atrophy and
reduce muscular load tolerance. Therefore, rehabilitation must progressively challenge the tendon across four distinct rehabilitation phases
while monitoring symptom response.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND TENDON MECHANOBIOLOGY
Tendons transmit high tensile forces from muscle to bone, acting as energetic springs during human locomotion. In the Achilles tendon,
ground reaction forces during running reach 6 to 8 times body weight, and up to 10 to 12 times body weight during sprinting and jumping.
Mechanical overload without adequate recovery induces microtrauma, tenocyte activation, upregulation of proteoglycans, and
disorganization of type I collagen fibers into weaker type III collagen bundles. Prolonged disuse or excessive rest further degrades tendon
tensile modulus, compounding the pathology. Mechanical stimulation within physiological strain windows (4-8% tendon strain) is required to
stimulate tenocyte collagen cross-linking and realignment.

THE PAIN-MONITORING MODEL
A central tenet of the proposed framework is the pain-monitoring model originally validated by Silbernagel et al. In this model, patients are
educated to rate their pain during and after exercise on a 0 to 10 Visual Analog Scale (VAS), where 0 represents no pain and 10 represents
the worst imaginable pain. Pain during physical loading and exercise is considered acceptable if it remains within the 0 to 5 range (mild to
moderate discomfort). Any pain exceeding 5 is categorized as unacceptable and requires an immediate reduction in exercise volume, load,
or speed.

Furthermore, the 24-hour response rule mandates that pain and morning stiffness the morning following loading must return to the
pre-exercise baseline level (VAS 0-2). If pain or morning stiffness remains elevated after 24 hours, the training volume or intensity was
excessive and must be deloaded until symptoms subside to baseline levels.

FOUR-PHASE RETURN-TO-SPORT REHABILITATION PROGRAM
Phase 1: Symptom Reduction and Activation. The primary objective of Phase 1 is to settle acute tendon irritability, manage pain, and initiate
triceps surae motor activation. Interventions include isometric plantar flexor holds (5 sets of 45 seconds at 70% of maximum voluntary
contraction) to provide immediate cortical analgesia, along with bilateral heel raises on flat ground progressing to single-leg heel raises (3
sets of 15 repetitions daily). Patients avoid high-velocity stretching or aggressive heel drops below the horizontal floor level.

Phase 2: Strength & Heavy Slow Resistance (HSR). Once single-leg heel raises on flat ground are tolerated with VAS <= 5, patients advance
to Phase 2. The objective is to build triceps surae muscle hypertrophy and tendon tensile stiffness. Exercises include single-leg heel raises
performed over the edge of a step through full available dorsiflexion and plantar flexion ROM with external resistance (using weighted vests,
barbells, or seated/standing calf machines). Load progresses from 3 sets of 15 RM to 3 sets of 6 to 8 RM at >= 70% to 85% 1RM, performed
every other day (3 sessions per week) with 48 hours of recovery between sessions.

Phase 3: Energy Storage & Plyometrics. When single-leg heel raise strength symmetry reaches >= 85% of the contralateral unaffected limb,
stretch-shortening cycle (SSC) exercises are introduced. Phase 3 targets tendon spring capacity and rapid energy storage and release.
Exercises progress from low-amplitude two-legged jumping, rope skipping, and bounding to high-intensity single-leg hops, zigzag hopping,
and deceleration drills. These sessions are performed 2 to 3 times weekly, always separated by at least 48 to 72 hours of recovery.

Phase 4: Return to Full Sport & Trail Running. Phase 4 prepares the athlete for unrestricted competitive sport, trail running, sprint
acceleration, and unpredictable multidirectional cutting. Athletes gradually increase running volume, interval speed, and downhill loading
while maintaining twice-weekly heavy resistance maintenance loading.

MECHANICAL STRAIN AND TENDON ADAPTATION PRINCIPLES
Achilles tendon structural remodeling requires a precise magnitude of cyclic mechanical strain, typically identified between 4% and 8%
tendon elongation. Low strain (<4%) fails to trigger tenocyte mechanotransduction, leaving matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) to outpace
tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs). Conversely, excessive mechanical strain (>8-10%) causes micro-tears in collagen cross-links
and stimulates inflammatory neurovascular proliferation. Heavy Slow Resistance (HSR) training delivers controlled 6-second repetitions (3
seconds concentric, 3 seconds eccentric) under heavy loads (70-85% 1RM), inducing continuous high-magnitude tensile deformation that
optimizes collagen synthesis rates without sudden peak impact stress.

MONITORING BIOMARKERS AND MORNING TENDON STIFFNESS
Morning stiffness upon taking the first steps out of bed serves as an invaluable surrogate biomarker of tendon tissue response to the
preceding day's mechanical loading. When an exercise session induces physiological remodeling within homeostatic tolerance, morning
stiffness resolves within 5 to 10 minutes (VAS <= 2). When mechanical load exceeds biological capacity, excessive proteoglycan hydration
and localized nociceptor sensitization produce stiffness lasting greater than 30 to 60 minutes with pain exceeding 3 on the VAS. Clinicians
must educate athletes to monitor this morning stiffness daily, using it as an objective trigger for load autoregulation: if stiffness is elevated,
the day's training volume is halved or converted to isometric holds.

CRITERIA FOR ADVANCEMENT AND RETURN TO SPORT CLEARANCE
Progression between phases must be guided by objective functional criteria rather than calendar weeks alone. Full sports clearance
requires: (1) full active ankle range of motion; (2) bilateral plantar flexor strength endurance symmetry >= 95% on the single-leg heel-raise
test; (3) hop test symmetry >= 90%; and (4) absence of pain exacerbation (> 5 VAS) or morning stiffness following sport-specific training.

DISCUSSION AND CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS
Tendons adapt more slowly than skeletal muscle due to lower vascularity and metabolic turnover rates. A minimum rehabilitation timeline of
12 to 24 weeks is required for midportion Achilles tendinopathy. Clinicians must resist rushing into energy storage activities before restoring
base plantar flexor strength. Continuing recreational sports under the pain-monitoring model preserves cardiovascular conditioning,

neuromuscular coordination, and psychological health without compromising tendon healing.
