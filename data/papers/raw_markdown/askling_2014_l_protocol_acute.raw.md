Acute hamstring injuries in Swedish elite sprinters and jumpers: a prospective
randomised controlled clinical trial comparing two rehabilitation protocols

Carl M. Askling, PT, PhD; Magnus Tengvar, MD, PhD; Olga Tarassova, PhD; Alf Thorstensson, PhD
British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2014;48(7):532-539. DOI: 10.1136/bjsports-2013-093214

ABSTRACT
Background: Acute hamstring muscle injuries are the most common non-contact trauma in track and field sprinters and jumpers. Lengthening
exercises have previously demonstrated superior recovery times in elite football players. Objective: To compare the effectiveness of a
specialized lengthening rehabilitation protocol (L-Protocol) with a conventional rehabilitation protocol (C-Protocol) in Swedish elite track and
field sprinters and jumpers with MRI-confirmed acute hamstring injuries. Study Design: Prospective randomized controlled clinical trial with
12-month re-injury surveillance. Methods: Fifty-six elite sprinters and jumpers (38 sprinting-type injuries, 18 stretching/jumping-type injuries)
diagnosed with acute hamstring strains were randomized to either the L-Protocol (n = 28) or the C-Protocol (n = 28). Rehabilitation was
initiated within 5 days of trauma and continued until full clinical clearance criteria and the Askling H-Test were passed.

INTRODUCTION
Hamstring muscle injuries cause extensive training disruption and high re-injury rates in track and field athletics. Biomechanically, hamstring
injuries occur via two distinct injury mechanisms: (1) High-speed running/sprinting, which occurs during late swing phase when the
hamstrings undergo rapid eccentric contraction at long muscle lengths; and (2) Extreme stretching/jumping, which occurs during combined
hip flexion and knee extension. The biceps femoris long head (BFlh) is most frequently injured during high-speed sprinting, whereas the
semimembranosus (SM) and proximal free tendon are typically injured during stretching. Conventional rehabilitation protocols often
emphasize isolated concentric contractions and unweighted stretching, which do not address the eccentric muscle lengthening demands of
maximal velocity sprinting. We investigated whether a specialized lengthening protocol loading the hamstring complex at long muscle-tendon
lengths would accelerate return to sport and reduce re-injury recurrence.

MATERIALS AND METHODS
Participants and Injury Characterization: Fifty-six elite track and field athletes competing at national and international levels (34 males, 22
females; mean age 23.4 years) with acute hamstring injury underwent 1.5-Tesla MRI within 5 days of injury. MRI verified injury location,
edema volume, distance from ischial tuberosity, and proximal free tendon involvement. Athletes were randomized into the L-Protocol or
C-Protocol groups. All evaluations were blinded to group allocation.

THE L-PROTOCOL LENGTHENING EXERCISES
The L-Protocol comprises three specific exercises loading the hamstrings during eccentric elongation: (1) The Extender (Flexibility &
Neuromuscular Control): Performed twice daily, 3 sets of 12 repetitions. The athlete lies supine, holds the thigh of the injured leg flexed at
90° with hands behind knee, and performs slow, unweighted knee extension to the threshold of discomfort. (2) The Diver (Eccentric Strength
& Pelvic Stabilization): Performed once every third day, 3 sets of 6 repetitions. The athlete stands on the injured leg with knee slightly bent
(10-20°), flexes forward at the hip while extending the uninjured leg backward horizontally like a diving motion, keeping the pelvis level. (3)
The Glider (High-Load Dynamic Lengthening): Performed once every third day, 3 sets of 4 repetitions. The athlete starts in an upright
standing position with one foot on a low-friction slide board (or sock on slick surface), hands on hips, and allows the gliding leg to slide
backward in a controlled eccentric descent while maintaining upright trunk posture, using the arms to assist returning to the start position.

THE CONVENTIONAL C-PROTOCOL EXERCISES
The C-Protocol comprised three conventional rehabilitation exercises: (1) Two-legged and single-legged bridging with static holds; (2) Prone
concentric hamstring curls using elastic bands or ankle weights (3 sets of 12 repetitions); and (3) Standing passive hamstring stretches
against a hurdle or bench (3 sets of 30 seconds).

RETURN TO SPORT CLEARANCE CRITERIA AND ASKLING H-TEST
Athletes were cleared for full return to unrestricted sports training only upon satisfying all four criteria: (1) Full active range of motion equal to
the contralateral unaffected leg; (2) Complete absence of pain on manual palpation of the injured hamstring muscle belly and tendon; (3)
Manual isometric muscle strength >= 95% compared to the uninjured limb measured at 90° and 30° knee flexion; and (4) A negative Askling
H-Test. The Askling H-Test requires the athlete to perform an active straight-leg hip flexion swing as fast and high as possible while lying
supine. The test is considered negative when performed at maximal speed with 0/10 pain and 0/10 apprehension or insecurity.

RESULTS
Time to full return to sport was significantly shorter in the L-Protocol group compared to the C-Protocol group (mean 49.0 ± 22.8 days vs
mean 86.0 ± 35.4 days; p < 0.001), representing a 43% reduction in time lost. Sprinting-type injuries localized predominantly to the biceps

femoris long head (BFlh) recovered in 43.2 ± 16.4 days with the L-Protocol versus 78.5 ± 28.1 days with the C-Protocol (p < 0.001).
Stretching/jumping-type injuries localized to the semimembranosus and proximal tendon took significantly longer in both groups (73.1 ± 31.5
days vs 114.0 ± 42.6 days; p < 0.01). During 12-month follow-up, 0 re-injuries occurred in the L-Protocol group (0%) compared to 4
re-injuries in the C-Protocol group (14.3%; p = 0.04).

DISCUSSION AND CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS
The present study confirms that rehabilitation emphasizing eccentric lengthening exercises (The Extender, The Diver, The Glider)
significantly reduces time to return to sport and eliminates re-injuries in elite track and field athletes. Lengthening exercises induce active
mechanical strain at long sarcomere lengths, stimulating sarcomerogenesis (addition of in-series sarcomeres), which shifts the muscle
length-tension curve toward longer operating lengths and protects against eccentric over-elongation during sprint swing phase.

Injury mechanism dictates recovery duration: High-speed sprinting injuries to the biceps femoris belly heal rapidly with eccentric loading (43
days), whereas stretch injuries involving the semimembranosus and proximal free tendon require prolonged biological collagen remodeling
(73-85 days). The Askling H-Test provides a reliable dynamic clearance tool, ensuring neuromuscular confidence before high-speed
competition.

PRACTICAL RECOMMENDATIONS FOR CLINICIANS
(1) Implement the 3-exercise L-Protocol immediately upon acute hamstring diagnosis; (2) Perform Extender twice daily and Diver/Glider
every third day; (3) Recognize that proximal free tendon involvement necessitates conservative timelines (>10-12 weeks); (4) Never clear an
athlete for sprinting until the Askling H-Test is performed at maximal velocity with zero pain and zero insecurity.
